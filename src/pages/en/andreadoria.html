Why this wreck

Two ships with radar, in the white

The Bismarck was hunted and killed by war. The Andrea Doria was not: it was peace, the fog, and two brand-new instruments read wrongly. The shipwreck that taught the world that radar, on its own, saves no one.

She was the pride of an Italy reborn from the war, a luxury liner Italians called a floating museum. One foggy night in 1956, off New York, she was torn open by the bow of another ship. Fifty-one died — almost all in the instant of the impact — but then something extraordinary happened: the ship stayed afloat long enough for everyone else, more than sixteen hundred people, to be saved. It is at once a tragedy and one of the greatest rescues in history. And her second life, on the bottom, is even more lethal than the first.

1953–1956 · the last age of the liners

The golden dusk of the great ships

In the mid-1950s crossing the Atlantic still means boarding an ocean liner: the great liners are at the peak of their splendour, just before the first jet aircraft make them obsolete within a decade. The Andrea Doria, in service from 1953, is the flagship of the Italian fleet: elegance, works of art, and the reputation of one of the safest ships ever built, with a double hull and watertight compartments.

She also had radar — the technology that, it was believed, had made collisions impossible.

It is precisely this confidence that is the seed of the disaster. Radar shows where the other ships are, but it does not think in the place of the one watching it. And on a night of thick fog, two crews who trusted their screens read the same scene in opposite ways.

Genoa, 1951 · how she was built

A museum that crossed the ocean

Before being a shipwreck, the Andrea Doria was a national pride. She was built by the Ansaldo yard in Genoa; she cost twenty-nine million dollars and bore the name of Andrea Doria, the great sixteenth-century Genoese admiral who had fought the Ottomans. When she entered service, in 1953, she was the floating calling card of an Italy risen from the war.

They called her "a floating museum" — and it was no figure of speech.

Every lounge was decorated with works of art commissioned from the best Italian artists: murals, ceramics, tapestries, and a life-size bronze statue of the admiral in the first-class lounge (it would be one of the first things divers tried to recover from the wreck). She was also the first liner with three open-air swimming pools, one for each class. She was not the largest or fastest ship on the Atlantic: she staked everything on elegance. And her clientele lived up to it — on that fatal voyage was the actress Betsy Drake, wife of Cary Grant.

The collision in the fog

The Andrea Doria is a few hours from New York, inbound from Genoa; the Swedish Stockholm has just left, bound for Europe. They approach in the fog, each seeing the other on radar. But their calculations of each other's course are wrong and mirror-imaged: both manoeuvre in the way that brings them together, instead of apart. Click to understand how.

Among the many fates of that night, the most incredible: Linda Morgan, fourteen, was asleep in her cabin on the Andrea Doria. The impact literally hurled her onto the smashed bow of the Stockholm, where she was found alive. Her sister and stepfather, in the same cabin, died.

The fatal flaw

Why she heeled over at once

A ship with a double hull and watertight compartments should not capsize from a single gash. The Andrea Doria did — and the reason is a technical detail the investigators put at the centre of the inquiry. At the end of the voyage her huge fuel tanks were nearly empty. The rules called for filling them with seawater as ballast, to keep the centre of gravity low; but then they had to be cleaned before refuelling — a cost and a nuisance. So, often, it was not done.

The result: as soon as the water came in through the gash, there was no weight on the struck side to balance it, and the ship heeled over sharply. Move the slider and watch what happens to the lifeboats.

Past fifteen degrees, the lifeboats on the high side — half the total — can no longer be lowered: they dangle against the hull. Within minutes the Andrea Doria had lost the use of half her boats. If the sea, that night, had not sent other ships, there would have been no way to save everyone. And it was not the first warning: on her maiden voyage the ship had already listed a full twenty-eight degrees from a single big wave.

The eleven hours

The great rescue

Listing on her side, with half the lifeboats jammed, the Andrea Doria sends the SOS. And the sea answers as it rarely does.

night of the 25th
The ships arrive
The Stockholm, though wounded, stays and lowers her boats. Other ships rush in from across the North Atlantic: freighters, a military ship, the U.S. Coast Guard.
the first hours
The Île de France turns back
The great French liner, already racing toward Europe, reverses course and returns to the scene of the disaster. She lights up every light in the fog and lowers her boats: it is she who saves the most survivors.
until dawn
Everyone else to safety
A shuttle of lifeboats ferries the passengers from the listing ship to the rescue ships, for hours. By dawn, of the more than 1,700 aboard, the living are all safe. Only the victims of the impact remain.
26 July · morning
She lies down and vanishes
Around 10 in the morning the Andrea Doria rolls onto her starboard side and slips beneath the surface, down to seventy-six metres. Her very slow agony is what made it possible to save everyone.
The second life, on the bottom

The "Mount Everest" of scuba diving

At seventy-six metres, the Andrea Doria is too deep for an ordinary dive and too alluring to be left in peace. She has become the dream and the nightmare of technical divers the world over: strong currents, cold dark water, sharp steel and nets to get tangled in. Click.

The ship

An elegance all Italian

About 213 metres long, built in Genoa, the Andrea Doria was a slender, elegant liner: a single great funnel in the colours of Italy, flowing lines, interiors decorated by artists. Not the largest or fastest ship of her time — but, for many, the most beautiful.

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not pass judgement on the blame. The inquiries and the lawsuits between the companies closed with a settlement before a verdict, and historians still argue over who manoeuvred worse. The page explains the mechanism — two opposite readings of the same radar — without assigning a blame the courts themselves did not fix.

And it does not turn an underwater grave into an invitation. The wreck is fascinating but it has killed eighteen people who went to visit it; here it is told as history and as a warning, not as a destination. The figures remain stated estimates.
