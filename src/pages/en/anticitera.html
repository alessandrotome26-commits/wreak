Why this wreck

What the sea gives back

The Titanic is the proof of what depth destroys. Antikythera is the proof of the opposite: now and then, the sea gives back — and once it gave back the most improbable object of antiquity.

In a Roman hold that sank while Rome was still fighting for the Mediterranean, among bronze statues and amphorae there was a wooden box the size of a dictionary. Inside, dozens of precision gears. It was a machine that calculated the sky: the positions of the Sun and the Moon, the lunar phases, the eclipses, even the calendar of the Olympic Games. Nothing comparable would reappear in the world for fourteen centuries. It is not the deepest wreck in this atlas, nor the richest. It is the one that rewrote the history of technology.

Spring 1900 · bound for Africa

The divers and the bronze corpses

A crew of sponge fishermen from the island of Symi, caught by a storm, drops anchor in the lee of the islet of Antikythera. Waiting for the sea to calm, the captain Dimitrios Kontos sends down a diver, Elias Stadiatis, in a canvas suit and copper helmet.

Stadiatis comes up terrified: on the bottom, he says, there is a heap of rotting corpses and horses.

They were not corpses. They were statues of bronze and marble, green and corroded by twenty centuries of sea, at forty-five to fifty metres down. The divers had found the cargo of a great Roman ship. From it, between 1900 and 1901, came the first great campaign of underwater archaeology in history, carried out with the Greek navy.

1900–1901 · the price

Salvaging at fifty metres, without knowing

No one, at the time, knew the physiology of decompression. The men went down to fifty metres, filled their nets with statues and came back up all at once. The toll was merciless: one diver died of decompression sickness and two were left paralysed. In the summer of 1901 the work stopped.

From the bottom, meanwhile, had emerged masterpieces: the Antikythera Ephebe, a bronze statue of a youth almost two metres tall, cast around 340 BC; heads of philosophers; fragments of horses; glass, jewels, coins. And, almost ignored amid the rest, an encrusted lump of bronze and wood, the size of a book.

1st century BC · Greece and Rome

A cargo bound for Rome

What was such a rich ship doing in that stretch of sea? The first century BC is the age in which Rome, now master of the Mediterranean, absorbs the Greek world — and with it its art. After Sulla's sack of Athens in 86 BC, Greek statues, bronzes and precious objects take the road to Italy en masse, to adorn the villas of a Roman aristocracy hungry for Hellenic culture.

The Antikythera ship was one of these: a merchantman laden with Greek treasures bound, in all likelihood, for wealthy Roman buyers.

And it is the detail that makes the mechanism even more extraordinary. Among statues and vases to show off travelled an instrument of pure science — the highest fruit of Greek astronomy, that of Hipparchus and the great schools of Rhodes. Not a drawing-room ornament: a machine for understanding the sky.

17 May 1902 · Athens

A gear in the stone

There was a toothed wheel inside the rock. FROM THE RECONSTRUCTION OF VALERIOS STAIS'S DISCOVERY

The lump, left to dry in a storeroom of the National Archaeological Museum, had split. The archaeologist Valerios Stais, examining it, noticed something that strictly could not exist: set into the corroded bronze was a toothed wheel, finely made, with Greek inscriptions all around.

It was unthinkable. A precision gear in a wreck of Roman age predated by over a thousand years everything believed about ancient mechanics. For decades it was dismissed as an enigma, perhaps a more recent intruder that had ended up there by chance. It took two more generations of scholars to prove that no: it was indeed from 70 BC, and it was a machine.

A crank, and the sky moves

Here is how it worked: you turn a side crank — representing the passing of time — and a train of gears moves the pointers of the Sun and the Moon along the ring of the zodiac, at different speeds. How we know

Reading a two-thousand-year-old machine with X-rays

For half a century the mechanism stayed unreadable: a corroded block that could not be opened without destroying it. The breakthrough came not from the screwdriver but from physics. In the 1970s the historian of science Derek de Solla Price grasped its gearwork; then the engineer Michael Wright built working models.

Since 2005 the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, led by Tony Freeth, has used high-resolution X-ray computed tomography to look inside the fragments: to count the teeth of every wheel and read thousands of Greek letters hidden in the corrosion — a true instruction manual, engraved. In 2021 the team published in Scientific Reports a complete model of the front dial, the "cosmos": Sun, Moon and the five planets on concentric rings. Two thousand years on, we are still learning to read it.

The vertigo

The gap of fourteen centuries

The problem with the mechanism is not that it is ancient. It is that it is alone: it has neither ancestors nor heirs.

An object of this precision does not come from nothing: it presupposes a tradition of workshops, of astronomers, of bronze craftsmen we do not know, because bronze is melted down and wood rots. And after it, the void: nothing comparable in mechanical complexity reappears until the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe, fourteen centuries later. Had the sea not by chance preserved this single example under fifty metres of water, we would not even know such a thing was possible. It is the sharpest reminder of how little of the past has come down to us.

The wreck that keeps giving

A hundred years of returns

1953 · 1976
Cousteau on the wreck
Jacques-Yves Cousteau visits the site with the Calypso, first in 1953 and then in 1976 with Greek permission. He recovers coins that help date the wreck to 70–60 BC, and other finds. Here too, a diver pays with the pains of decompression.
2012 →
Return to Antikythera
A Greek-international mission returns to the wreck with modern technology — technical divers, robots, even an armoured "exosuit". The seabed is far from exhausted.
2016 · 2022
A skeleton, and more statues
In 2016 the skeleton of one of the wreck's victims emerges, nicknamed Pamphilos, with potentially analysable DNA. In 2022, new fragments of statues. More than a century after the discovery, the Antikythera ship still gives back.

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not pass off hypotheses as certainties. That the mechanism showed all five planets is the most accredited reconstruction, but it is a reconstruction: here it is stated as such. The number of gears is the surviving one, not the original. The dating is "about 70–60 BC", and so it stays.

It does not treat the mechanism as magic or as proof of alien visits — the nonsense that piles up around it. It is the product, extraordinary but human, of a Greek technical tradition of which we have lost almost everything. And the simulator on this page is avowedly a model of the principle, not a replica of the real clockwork.
