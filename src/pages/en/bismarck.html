Why this wreck

Eight days, and the bottom of the ocean

The Mary Rose we brought back up. The Bismarck, no: she lies at nearly five thousand metres, the deepest wreck in this atlas, and there she will stay. Her only mission lasted eight days — and held the world's breath.

She was one of the largest battleships ever built, the symbol of German naval power. In those few days of May 1941 she sank the proudest ship in the Royal Navy and was then hunted down and destroyed by an entire fleet. It is the story of a hunt — and of a grave: about two thousand men died with her. This atlas tells it as a historical event and as a place of burial, without glorifying the war machine or the regime that sent it to sea.

May 1941 · the Atlantic

The war of the convoys

In 1941 Britain depends for its very survival on the convoys crossing the Atlantic: food, fuel, weapons. Cutting that line is the German objective. With Operation Rheinübung, the Bismarck and the cruiser Prinz Eugen break out into the open sea to hunt the merchantmen: a single great battleship loose in the ocean can paralyse the traffic of an empire.

Stopping her becomes, for the Royal Navy, a matter of life and death — and of prestige.

Against the two German ships Britain deploys a huge part of its fleet. The first clash, however, begins badly: very badly.

24 May 1941 · Denmark Strait

The end of the Hood

At dawn on 24 May the Bismarck meets the pride of the Royal Navy: the battlecruiser HMS Hood, for twenty years the symbol of British naval power, together with the battleship Prince of Wales. The clash lasts minutes. A salvo from the Bismarck reaches the Hood's magazines.

Sink the Bismarck. WINSTON CHURCHILL'S ORDER, AFTER THE NEWS OF THE LOSS OF THE HOOD

The Hood explodes and breaks in two. In minutes she goes down with almost her entire crew: of the 1,418 men aboard, three survive. It is a terrible blow to British morale — and from that moment the hunt becomes relentless.

24–27 May · the hunt

Three thousand kilometres of pursuit

Hit in turn and leaking fuel, the Bismarck heads for the ports of occupied France to repair. The Royal Navy chases her across the storming Atlantic. Follow the stages.

1 · The break-out
On 18–22 May the Bismarck and Prinz Eugen leave Germany and Norway and head into the Atlantic through the Denmark Strait, to slip past the British blockade and fall on the convoys.
26 May · the blow that decides everything

The biplane that killed a battleship

Having eluded her pursuers for a whole day, the Bismarck seems within reach of safety. Then, at dusk, from the carrier Ark Royal take off some Fairey Swordfish: biplanes of canvas and wood, slow and already obsolete. They seem incapable of scratching a steel hull. One of them, instead, will change history.

But there is a detail that nearly ruined everything. The first wave of Swordfish attacked HMS Sheffield by mistake, a British cruiser, taking her for the Bismarck. Luckily their torpedoes, fitted with magnetic detonators, exploded too early or missed: no damage. But that failure revealed that the magnetic detonators were faulty — so the second wave fitted contact detonators. And that is why, a few hours later, it struck for real. Click.

27 May · the end

Ninety minutes of fire

On the morning of 27 May the British battleships King George V and Rodney, with the cruisers, reach the immobilized Bismarck and open fire. In a little over an hour and a half they pour nearly three thousand shells into her. The Bismarck is reduced to a burning wreck — but does not sink.

And here an enigma is born that is still open: who really sent her to the bottom?

The British claim her as their prize; the German survivors have always maintained that they scuttled her, opening the sea valves so as not to hand her to the enemy. When she finally goes down, around 10:40, hundreds of men remain in the water. The British ships pick up about 110, then break off the rescue over a submarine alarm, leaving very many to die. Of the roughly two thousand two hundred aboard, little more than a hundred are saved.

8 June 1989 · nearly five thousand metres

Found by the man who found the Titanic

The same hand that had located the Titanic found the Bismarck too: in 1989 Robert Ballard located her at about 4,790 metres, hundreds of kilometres west of France. And here the page descends with her, from the grey of the storm to the dark of the abyss.

The wreck stands upright on the bottom and is surprisingly intact: the hull has not collapsed as one would expect of a ship sunk by enemy fire alone. For many scholars it is the clue that weighs in favour of scuttling — though almost all agree that the battle damage would have doomed her anyway. Ballard touched nothing: he regarded her, like the Titanic, as a place of burial to be respected.

The ship

What she was

Entering service in 1940, the Bismarck was about 251 metres long and displaced, fully loaded, around fifty thousand tons: one of the largest battleships ever built in Europe. Eight 380 mm guns in four turrets, an imposing tower superstructure amidships, a single funnel.

A persistent legend has it that the ship's cat also survived the sinking, later nicknamed "Unsinkable Sam", picked up by a British ship and surviving — it is said — two more sinkings that same year. There is no solid evidence tying that cat to the Bismarck: it is a fine sea-tale, and as such we leave it.

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not glorify a war machine or the regime that built it. The Bismarck was an instrument of Nazi Germany, and the technical fascination of her story does not erase that. The page treats her as a historical event and as a grave — of both sides: the 1,415 dead of the Hood and the roughly two thousand of the Bismarck deserve the same respect.

And it does not close a question still open. Whether British shells or German scuttling sent her to the bottom is debated among historians; the page lays out the clues and leaves the doubt where it is. The figures remain stated estimates.
