Why this wreck

The shipwreck everyone lived through

In an atlas of lost ships, the Endurance is the luminous exception: the story is not of how many died, but of how twenty-eight men out of twenty-eight came home after the sea had taken their ship.

Then there is the second life, just as improbable. Crushed by the ice in 1915, the ship sank to three thousand metres in one of the most remote seas on the planet, and there she stayed invisible for a hundred and seven years. When robots finally lit her up in 2022, she was no corroded skeleton: she was a ship, standing on the bottom, with her name still legible on the stern. The same sea that had killed her had also preserved her — for the same reason as the Vasa, at the other end of the world.

1914 · the ambition

To cross Antarctica on foot

With the South Pole reached by Amundsen in 1911, one last feat remained: to cross the entire Antarctic continent by land, sea to sea, passing through the Pole. Ernest Shackleton made it his own. The ship chosen to carry the team to the edge of the Weddell Sea was a three-masted oak vessel built in Norway, launched as Polaris and renamed Endurance after Shackleton's family motto: Fortitudine vincimus, "by endurance we conquer".

Aboard, twenty-eight men and nearly seventy sled dogs. But they never reached the continent: the ice stopped them before they could even land.

August 1914 · on the brink of war

Setting off as the world goes into the trenches

There is a detail always forgotten: the Endurance sailed just as Europe was plunging into the First World War. In August 1914, with war just declared, Shackleton offered ship and crew to the war effort. From the Admiralty, Winston Churchill replied with a single word: "Proceed."

So the purest of adventures set off for the ice at the very moment the continent was sinking into the mud of the trenches.

It is a contrast that gives the measure of the enterprise: while millions of men went to die in Europe, twenty-eight of them went to stake their lives at the opposite end of the planet for a geographic first. And several of those Antarctica gave back alive would later end up fighting for real.

January–November 1915 · Weddell Sea

Caught, carried, crushed

In January 1915, a day's sailing from the coast, the pack ice closes around the hull and holds it fast. The Endurance stops being a ship and becomes a floating wreck: a prisoner of the ice, she drifts with it for months, dragged slowly to the northwest.

What the ice gets, the ice keeps. A LINE ATTRIBUTED TO ERNEST SHACKLETON, FACING THE GRIP OF THE PACK

In October the pressure of the ice begins to break the hull; water comes in. On 21 November 1915 the ship slips beneath the surface. Before leaving her, Captain Frank Worsley — an extraordinary navigator — notes the position of the sinking. It would be that number, a century later, that guided those who came looking for her.

1915–1916 · the escape

Eight hundred miles not to die

With no ship, no radio, on a drifting floe, twenty-eight men begin a retreat that has become legend. Follow the stages.

1 · The drift in the ice
From January 1915 the pack imprisons the Endurance and drags her northwest for ten months, until she sinks on 21 November. The crew camps on the ice and waits for it to break up.
The stages of the escape

How to come back from nothing

April 1916
Elephant Island, in three boats
When the pack breaks up, the twenty-eight cram into the three boats saved from the ship — the James Caird, the Dudley Docker, the Stancomb Wills — and after days in the open sea reach an uninhabited, wind-swept island. It is solid ground, the first in sixteen months. But no one will ever pass that way.
April–May 1916
The James Caird, 800 miles of ocean
Shackleton picks five men and the best of the boats, seven metres long. They cross about 800 nautical miles of the stormiest sea on Earth, guided only by Worsley's rare sextant readings, to South Georgia. Fifteen, sixteen days. One of the greatest feats of seamanship in history.
May 1916
On foot over the mountains
Landed on the wrong side of the island, Shackleton, Worsley and Crean cross on foot the never-traversed mountains of South Georgia — thirty-six hours without a stop — and reach the whaling station at Stromness.
30 August 1916
No one left behind
After four attempts turned back by the ice, Shackleton returns to Elephant Island on the Chilean tug Yelcho and picks up the twenty-two men left there. They were all alive. In two years of disaster, not one death.
The images

The man who saved the photographs from the ice

That images of this story exist at all is thanks to one man: Frank Hurley, the expedition photographer. When the ship was abandoned, he dived into the freezing water of the flooded hold to recover the glass photographic plates. Then, having to lighten the load, he and Shackleton selected about a hundred to keep and smashed the rest on the ice, so that no one would be tempted to go back for them.

Those surviving plates are among the most extraordinary photographs ever taken: the ship lit up at night, trapped and already doomed, in the middle of the white.

5 March 2022 · under the ice

Three thousand metres down, a century later

Finding her seemed impossible: the Weddell Sea is covered by the most hostile pack ice on the planet, the very ice that had stopped the Endurance herself. The Endurance22 expedition, sailing from Cape Town on the South African icebreaker S.A. Agulhas II, lowered autonomous robots — the Sabertooth — beneath the ice, with only the position Worsley noted in 1915 as a starting point.

They found her on 5 March 2022, about four miles from that point, at 3,008 metres. And here the page descends with her: from the white of the pack to the absolute dark of the bottom, where the ship rests upright.

By far the finest wooden shipwreck I have ever seen. She is upright, well clear of the seabed, intact. MENSUN BOUND, DIRECTOR OF EXPLORATION, ENDURANCE22

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not inflate the numbers of an epic already extraordinary on its own. The "about 800 miles" of the James Caird and the "about ten months" of drift remain approximations, as the sources report them. The wreck position is the one stated by the 2022 expedition, not a coordinate to the metre: the site is protected and its integrity comes before curiosity.

It does not reduce the survival to luck or to pure heroism. It was a combination of discipline, exceptional navigation and the right decisions taken at the right moment — and also, it must be said, of a margin that could have broken at a thousand points. That it ended without a death was by no means written.
