Why this wreck

Not a treasure. A whole day, frozen.

The USS Arizona is a grave we choose not to touch. The Mary Rose is its opposite: a wreck we brought back up and learned to read — the richest time capsule of everyday sixteenth-century life.

She carried no gold. She carried the ordinary life of five hundred men, sealed by the mud for over four centuries: the bows they fought with, the surgeon's chest, the musical instruments, the combs, the dice, the shoes, even the ship's dog. And their own bones. No document will ever tell us about the sixteenth century what this wreck tells — because here there is not the History of kings, but the working day of ordinary men, cut short one summer afternoon in 1545.

1509–1545 · the king and his ships

The navy of an ambitious king

The Mary Rose was one of Henry VIII's favourite ships, launched in 1511 at the dawn of his reign. She was among the first European warships built for a new idea of fighting: no longer only to ram and board, but to open a row of ports along the side and unleash a broadside of heavy cannon. A revolution that would rule the seas for three centuries.

But the ports that made her powerful were also her Achilles' heel: set too low, they let the sea in.

In the summer of 1545 England is under threat of invasion. Francis I of France launches against the English coast an enormous fleet — some three hundred ships and thirty-eight thousand men, larger than the Spanish Armada forty-three years later. Blocking their way, in the Solent, is Henry's fleet. And the Mary Rose is in the front line.

19 July 1545 · the Solent

She sank before the king's eyes

That afternoon Henry VIII himself watches the battle from Southsea Castle. The Mary Rose is manoeuvring to face the French galleys when — perhaps from a sudden gust, perhaps from an overload, perhaps from the crew's indiscipline — she heels sharply in a turn. The lower gun ports are open. The sea comes in.

I have the sort of knaves I cannot rule. WORDS ATTRIBUTED TO CAPTAIN SIR GEORGE CAREW SHORTLY BEFORE THE SINKING, FROM A CONTEMPORARY ACCOUNT

The ship fills and goes down in minutes. And there is a detail that turns the sinking into a slaughter: over the deck was stretched an anti-boarding net, to stop the enemy from climbing aboard. That same net, as the ship sinks, traps the crew beneath it. Of the roughly five hundred men, fewer than forty are saved. The captain, Sir George Carew, drowns with his ship.

1971–1982 · found and raised

Bringing her back up

The mud of the Solent had buried and sealed half the hull, preserving it for centuries. In 1971 the diver Alexander McKee found it; then the Mary Rose Trust, with the archaeologist Margaret Rule, ran one of the largest underwater campaigns ever made — tens of thousands of dives. The people

The men themselves

With the hull came up also the remains of about one hundred and eighty crew. And the bones speak. The skeletons of the archers show characteristic deformations at the shoulder and back: the body of someone who spent his life drawing bows of seventy kilos of pull, from childhood.

But the most surprising discovery came recently: that crew was not English at all.

Chemical analyses of teeth and bones show that several men had grown up in warmer climates — from southern Europe, perhaps from North Africa. The Mary Rose was not a ship of only fair-haired Englishmen: it was a small cosmopolitan world, as Tudor England's ports really were. Together with them came up Hatch too, the dog, the most silent and most eloquent witness of all.

Real historical image

The only depiction of the Mary Rose made by someone who had seen her is in the Anthony Roll, the illustrated inventory of Henry VIII's fleet, painted in 1546 — a year after the sinking.

The ship

What she looked like at sea

The Mary Rose was a carrack: tall castles fore and aft, four masts with square and lateen sails, and the long coloured Tudor streamers in the wind. After the 1536 upgrade she displaced around seven hundred tons and mounted up to eighty guns.

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not settle on a single cause for the sinking. A gust of wind, an overload, a wrong manoeuvre, indiscipline: sources and scholars still argue, and here the doubt stays written instead of vanishing into a single explanation. The crew figures are stated as estimates.

And it does not confuse the value of this wreck with that of a treasure. The Mary Rose is not worth anything for gold — she had none. She is worth it because she gives us back, object by object and bone by bone, the real life of five hundred ordinary people of five centuries ago. It is the kind of wealth no coin can buy.
