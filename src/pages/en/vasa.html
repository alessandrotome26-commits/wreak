Why this wreck

The only one you walk through

The Atocha is told. The Vasa is visited: she is the only seventeenth-century ship in the world preserved almost whole, standing inside a museum built around her.

From the 1961 recovery to the start of 2025, more than 45 million people have seen her. But the reason this card comes right after the Atocha is another: she is its exact opposite. The Atocha carried a treasure and sailed 100 miles; the Vasa carried nothing and sailed less than one. The Atocha is still scattered on the seabed; the Vasa came back up, whole. One story goes down. The other rises.

1611–1628 · a power is born

The Sweden that wanted the Baltic

The Vasa was not born of a monarch's whim, but of the ambition of a rising nation. In the early seventeenth century the Sweden of Gustavus Adolphus is turning from a peripheral kingdom into a great military power: it fights Poland-Lithuania for control of the Baltic coasts and prepares to enter the Thirty Years' War, the conflict tearing Europe apart.

The Baltic is the arena, and whoever dominates it dominates the trade of the North. Sweden wants the most powerful fleet on the sea.

It is in this arms race that the king orders a warship like no other: two gun decks, a forest of gilded sculptures, a floating symbol of the realm's power. The problem is that ambition runs faster than the naval engineering of the age — and the bill will be paid by the ship.

1626 · Skeppsgården, Stockholm

The king's ship

Sweden is at war and Gustavus Adolphus wants the most powerful ship in the Baltic: two gun decks, 64 cannons, over seven hundred gilded sculptures telling the glory of the Vasa dynasty. The Dutch master shipwright Henrik Hybertsson dies during construction. The specifications change midway, under pressure from a king who is in a hurry and away at the front.

The result is splendid, very tall — and with all the weight in the wrong place: up high.

Summer 1628 · the test no one wanted to see

Thirty men running

Before departure the captain Söfring Hansson has a stability test carried out: thirty men run from one side of the deck to the other. After three passes the ship rolls so much that the admiral present stops the test, for fear she will capsize right there, at her mooring.

The officers would like to warn the king. The king is not in Stockholm. The problem is ignored and the departure confirmed.

Sunday 10 August 1628 · late afternoon

Twenty minutes

The departure
Four sails of ten
The Vasa leaves her mooring beneath the Tre Kronor castle, fires the salute, and the crowd on the quays applauds. Aboard, together with the crew, there are women and children: the families have been invited for the first stretch.
The first gust
The ship heels
A light gust tips her onto her port side. She rights herself with difficulty. The crowd falls silent.
The second gust
Water pours in through the open ports
The gun ports of the lower deck are wide open for the salute. The ship heels past the limit, the Baltic pours in through the ports, and in a few minutes the Vasa sinks at Tegelviken, in front of all Stockholm. She has sailed about 1,300 metres.
32 metres
The masts stay above the water
About thirty people die, trapped inside the hull. The bottom is so shallow that the mast tops stick out of the surface: they will be sawn off.
The inquiry
God alone knows THE ANSWER GIVEN AT THE INQUIRY INTO THE CAUSE OF THE SINKING

The captain is arrested and then released: the ship had been built to the approved specifications — and the one who had approved them was the king. The builder had died two years earlier. In the end no one is punished. It is one of the oldest documented cases of an engineering disaster in which the blame, climbing the chain, arrives too high to stop on anyone.

1663–1665 · thirty-five years later

The diving bell

The first chapter of the recovery is from the seventeenth century, and it is almost incredible: salvagers, breathing the air trapped in a diving bell, descend to 32 metres in the dark and cold, and bring back most of the bronze cannons — the ship's real economic value. It is one of the earliest documented diving salvage operations in history.

Then, stripped of its bronze, the Vasa ceases to interest anyone. Its position is lost. The ship becomes a legend.

333 years

The wrong sea to sink in, the right one to last

The Vasa survived thanks to a detail of biology: Teredo navalis, the mollusc that devours wooden wrecks in every sea, does not live in the Baltic.

Cold water, low salt, an oxygen-poor mud bottom: the combination that in any ocean would have left a skeleton of frames has here preserved a hull. If the Atocha had sunk in Stockholm and the Vasa in Florida, this card would not exist today and the Atocha's would show a whole ship.

25 August 1956

A piece of black oak

Anders Franzén, a naval engineer and amateur archaeologist, had been searching for the Vasa for years with a core sampler he built himself, pulling up — in his words — rusty stoves, women's bicycles, Christmas trees and dead cats. The decisive clue comes from surveys for a bridge: an anomalous rise on the seabed off the island of Beckholmen.

On 25 August 1956 the core sampler comes up with a plug of blackened oak. In September the divers confirm it: it is her.

1957–1961 · the raising

Eighteen lifts

1957–1959
Six tunnels under the hull
Divers of the Swedish navy dig six galleries in the clay under the ship, at 32 metres, with zero visibility, using water jets, to pass the steel cables connected to the pontoons.
August 1959
The first pull
No one knows whether the hull will hold. It holds. In eighteen successive lifts the Vasa is moved to shallower water, where she is patched and sealed for the final leap.
24 April 1961 · 9:03 am
The seventeenth century resurfaces
On live television, before the world, the planking breaks the surface with mud pouring from the ports. Once the ports are sealed and the water pumped out, the Vasa floats: she enters the dock on her own keel, 333 years later.
The second life

Keeping her dry is harder than raising her

Wood soaked with water for three centuries collapses in the air. To prevent this the ship is sprayed for years, day and night, with polyethylene glycol — a wax that takes the place of the water in the fibres. First a temporary museum, the Wasavarvet; then, at the end of the 1980s, the move into the purpose-built building: the Vasamuseet.

Conservation is not over: it is permanent work, and the Vasa is today one of the most important laboratories in the world on how to preserve archaeological wood. Even standing still, this ship keeps teaching.

The people

Those who were aboard

Between 150 and 250 people, including the family members invited for the first stretch of the voyage. About thirty did not get out of the hull, and their remains resurfaced with the ship: the DNA studies of 2004 identified at least fifteen adults, and the museum has reconstructed some faces.

One of those remains, catalogued for decades under the convenient name "Gustav", turned out on genetic analysis to be a woman.

It is the kind of correction only a preserved wreck allows: here archaeology does not reconstruct a story — it meets it, person by person. No other card in this atlas will be able to say the same.

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not hide the position: there is nothing to protect here, the ship is in a public museum and the point of the sinking is known history. The atlas's rule stays the same as the Atocha's, though — the discretion over coordinates applies to sites still on the bottom, and every card states which case it is.

It does not pass estimates off as counts: the dead are "about thirty", the people aboard "between 150 and 250", and so they stay written. And the stability simulator is avowedly a simplified model, not naval engineering.
