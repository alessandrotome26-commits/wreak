Why this wreck

The sinking that changed the war

The Titanic was a tragedy of pride: an accident. The Lusitania was something different — an act of war against civilians, and for that reason it changed history more than almost any other wreck.

She is not the deepest nor the deadliest in this atlas. But a single torpedo, a second explosion never fully explained and eighteen minutes were enough to kill nearly twelve hundred people, among them one hundred and twenty-eight Americans. From there came a wave of outrage, a propaganda war, and the long slide of the United States toward the First World War. This card tells how a ship that sank in under twenty minutes shifted the centre of gravity of the twentieth century.

May 1915 · the warning

A notice next to the advertisement

By May 1915 the First World War had entered its darkest phase. Germany had declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone, where any enemy ship could be sunk without warning by its U-boats. And it made that known.

On 1 May, in the New York papers, a notice from the German embassy appeared — with a cruel twist of fate — right beside the Cunard advertisement for the Lusitania's departure.

It reminded travellers that anyone boarding a British ship in those waters did so at their own risk. But the Lusitania was the fastest ship on the Atlantic: no one truly believed a slow submarine could ever catch her. She sailed with nearly two thousand people aboard.

One torpedo, then something else

A few kilometres from the Irish coast, the submarine U-20, commanded by Walther Schwieger, had the most famous ship in the world in its sights. It fired a single torpedo. It struck below the waterline, toward the bow.

An unusually heavy detonation, with a great cloud of smoke far above the funnel. FROM THE LOG OF U-20, JUST AFTER THE TORPEDO STRUCK

It was that second explosion — far more violent than the torpedo — that doomed the ship. The Lusitania listed at once, and so sharply that almost all the lifeboats became unusable: only six of the forty-eight were launched. In eighteen minutes the fastest ship in the world was on the bottom. But what really caused that second blast? It is the mystery that has lasted a century.

The mystery

The people

Who was aboard

Among the nearly two thousand passengers were emigrant families, infants, and some of America's best-known names. The most famous is Alfred Vanderbilt, one of the richest men in the United States. He could not swim. In the last minutes he helped others into the lifeboats and, according to witnesses, gave his own life jacket to a woman. His body was never found.

The captain, William Turner, survived — and paid a price for it.

"Bowler Bill", as he was nicknamed, stayed on the bridge to the last, and it was the buoyancy of his life jacket alone that saved him. Cleared of every charge — he had stayed at his post — he nonetheless became the scapegoat of a nation looking for someone to blame, and lived the rest of his life in semi-seclusion.

The war of images

How a medal became propaganda

The Lusitania did not sink only in the water: she sank in world public opinion, and there the battle went on. The most curious episode concerns a small object. A German artist, Karl Goetz, struck a satirical medal accusing Cunard of loading munitions onto a passenger ship, with the mocking legend "no contraband".

The British seized the chance. They reversed the sense of the satire and had about two hundred and fifty thousand copies made, sold to the public as proof of German barbarity. An object created to justify the sinking ended up as one of the most effective propaganda weapons against Germany.

The consequence

Two years until the war

It is often said, for convenience, that the Lusitania "brought America into the war". It is not true, and it is right to say so precisely: the United States entered the conflict only in April 1917, nearly two years later, when Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann telegram was intercepted.

But the Lusitania did something deeper: it shifted the feeling of a whole country.

It showed an America that regarded the war as a distant, European affair just how ruthless Germany was prepared to be. Those one hundred and twenty-eight American dead did not bring a declaration of war, but they made thinkable what had been unthinkable. When the war came, "Remember the Lusitania" was already written on the walls.

The ship

The fastest on the Atlantic

Entering service in 1907, the Lusitania was about 240 metres long, with four unmistakable funnels in Cunard's colours, red-orange and black. She won the Blue Riband for the fastest Atlantic crossing: it was her very speed that fed the belief — proven fatal — that no submarine could ever threaten her.

A note on method

What this page does not do

It does not close an unsolved mystery. What caused the second explosion — munitions, coal, boilers — has been debated for a century, and the page presents the theories with their real weight instead of choosing the most sensational one. The manifest did list war materiel: that is a fact, and it should be stated without thereby justifying the sinking of a ship full of civilians.

And it does not simplify the consequences. The Lusitania did not "bring America into the war": it pushed her, prepared her, made it thinkable — two years before it actually happened. Distinguishing influence from cause is the kind of precision this story deserves. The figures remain stated estimates.

The next one has no explanation: the Mary Celeste

From the mystery of a single explosion to a total mystery. In 1872 the Mary Celeste was found adrift in the middle of the Atlantic, in perfect order, cargo intact and the lifeboat gone — but with not a soul aboard. The crew was never seen again, and no one, to this day, knows what happened.
