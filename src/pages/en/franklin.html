Why this wreck

The greatest polar mystery — and who had the answer

Two ships of the most powerful navy in the world, one hundred and twenty-nine men, swallowed by the ice without a single white witness. For a century and a half, the Empire looked for the truth in the wrong place.

Because the truth already existed, and had been told: the Inuit had seen it, knew it, and said so. Only, no one in London would believe "savages". It took one hundred and seventy years and the discovery of the two wrecks — exactly where Inuit oral tradition had pointed — for that testimony to be recognised as accurate. This card tells two things at once: the chilling end of the Franklin expedition, and the long injustice done to those who, first of all, had told the truth.

May 1845 · the departure

The pride of an empire, armoured in certainty

The Northwest Passage — a sea route through the Arctic linking Atlantic and Pacific — was the obsession of the British navy. In 1845 the Admiralty assembled the best-equipped expedition ever mounted: two sturdy bomb vessels, the Erebus and the Terror, reinforced against the ice, fitted with steam engines, heated cabins, a library of over a thousand volumes and tinned food for three years.

In command, the fifty-nine-year-old Sir John Franklin. Aboard, one hundred and twenty-nine men and the certainty of winning.

That technology, however, hid its own snares. The tinned food, soldered with lead, may have slowly poisoned the crews; and no steam engine could do anything against an Arctic that, that year, never released the ships from its grip. Some whalers sighted them in Baffin Bay in July 1845. Then, silence.

1846–1848 · the slow end

One hundred and twenty-nine ways not to come home

The ships stayed trapped in the pack off King William Island for almost two winters. The men died one by one: scurvy, hunger, cold, perhaps lead poisoning. Franklin died on 11 June 1847. In April 1848 the one hundred and five survivors abandoned the ships and attempted the impossible: to march south on foot, dragging boats mounted on sledges, in search of a river that would carry them to an outpost.

None of them made it. All one hundred and twenty-nine died.

The bones found along the line of the march tell the last, terrible chapter: cut marks consistent with cannibalism, the final resort of desperate men. It was the part of the truth that Victorian England would reject most violently.

The only document

The sheet that tells the truth twice

Of the whole expedition, a single, harrowing written document survives: a note left in a cairn at Victory Point. It was filled in twice, a year apart, on the same sheet. Use the switch.

H.M.S. EREBUS & TERROR — VICTORY POINT
28th of May 1847. HM Ships Erebus and Terror have wintered in the ice. Sir John Franklin commands the expedition. All well.
Gore, lieutenant · Des Vœux, mate
May 1847. All is going to plan. The words "All well" close the message: no one aboard imagines what is about to come.

The same sheet, taken up eleven months later, became the expedition's tombstone: the officers added the new tidings in the blank spaces, in the margins of the old, optimistic message. It is the before and after of a catastrophe, on a single piece of paper.

1854 · the truth, refused

"Savages cannot be right"

While dozens of relief expeditions combed the Arctic in vain, one explorer, John Rae, did the simplest and wisest thing: he listened to the Inuit. In 1854 he gathered their accounts — precise, consistent — of the death of Franklin's men, and even of the final cannibalism. He brought the material proof to London: cutlery bearing the officers' initials, Sir John's own medal.

From the mutilated state of many of the bodies, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence. FROM JOHN RAE'S REPORT TO THE ADMIRALTY, 1854 — BASED ON INUIT TESTIMONY

The reaction was ferocious. The widow, Lady Jane Franklin, could not accept that a hero of the Empire had ended like this, and pressed the most celebrated writer of the day, Charles Dickens, to demolish Rae in public. Dickens wrote that "civilised" British officers would never have stooped to cannibalism, and branded the Inuit as lying, murderous "savages". Rae, who had simply told the truth, was sidelined and never honoured as he deserved.

2014–2016 · the testimony vindicated

The ships were where the Inuit said

The story has an ending that tastes of justice. An Inuit researcher, Louie Kamookak, spent thirty years gathering the oral memory of his community's elders about the places of the expedition. When Parks Canada began the modern search, it was his knowledge that pointed to where to look.

In 2014 the Erebus was found. In 2016 the Terror — in the bay that bears her name, exactly where Inuit tradition had always placed her.

The two ships lie in cold, shallow water, extraordinarily preserved: whole decks, objects in their place, a submerged archive still to be read. But the most important discovery is not made of wood: it is that an Indigenous testimony, mocked for a hundred and seventy years as a tale of "savages", proved to be the most precise map we had. The site is now co-managed with the Inuit, and their voice, at last, is at the centre of the story.

The ships

Two bomb vessels, gripped by the pack

Erebus and Terror were unusual warships — bomb vessels, built to withstand the recoil of huge mortars — and precisely for that reason fitted with extremely strong hulls, ideal to reinforce for the ice. Terror herself had already faced the Antarctic. What lingers in the imagination is the scene of their end: two three-masters motionless, prisoners of the pack, under the green light of the aurora borealis.

A note on method

Who has the right to tell the story

This card takes a stand on one thing only: on who was right. For a century and a half the official version trusted the written documents of the Empire and treated Inuit oral memory as unreliable on principle. The wrecks, found where the Inuit said, overturned that hierarchy. To acknowledge it is not sentimentality: it is method. A source must be judged by its accuracy, not by the skin colour of whoever passes it on.

On the rest, the page stays cautious: the question of lead is debated and we present it as such, while cannibalism is an osteological fact, not a moral accusation. The wrecks are graves and archives at once, co-managed with the Inuit communities — and they are to be looked at, not plundered. The greatest lesson of Franklin is not how one dies in the ice, but how, for far too long, it was decided whom to believe.
