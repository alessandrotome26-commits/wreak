Why this wreck

The oldest proof that the world was already connected

An Arab ship, a Chinese cargo, an Indonesian wreck. In a single wreck three civilizations meet, eleven centuries ago — and the surprise is how networked the world already was.

In the ninth century, while Europe was fragmented, two great empires — Tang China and the Abbasid caliphate of Baghdad — were linked by a trade route that crossed half the planet by sea. We knew of that route from texts. Then, in 1998, Indonesian fishermen found the ship: an Arab dhow sunk with sixty thousand Chinese objects in its hold. For the first time we could study, plank by plank, the machinery of the first globalization. And on one bowl, incredibly, a date was written.

The ship

A ship sewn, not nailed

The most surprising thing was not in the hold, but in the hull. The Belitung dhow had not a single iron nail: its planks were sewn together with plant-fibre cord, by an ancient Arab technique that made the hull flexible and repairable anywhere. It was made of Indian teak and African woods — so even its construction was a weave of three continents.

It is the first Arab dhow ever found in Southeast Asian waters.

Ships like this lived on the breath of the monsoons: they left the Persian Gulf with the seasonal wind, reached the ports of southern China such as Guangzhou — where there were whole quarters of foreign merchants — and returned when the wind reversed, holds full of goods. This ship had completed the outward voyage. It sank on the way back, a step from its owners' home.

The first globalization

Fifty-six thousand bowls, all alike

The number that strikes most is not the value of the treasures, but the quantity of the ordinary pieces: about fifty-six thousand five hundred near-identical bowls, from the Changsha kilns in the Chinese interior. They were not works of art for the elite: they were goods, mass-produced, stacked in columns inside large jars to survive the voyage.

It is the material proof of an already global economy: a Chinese factory producing non-stop for customers ten thousand kilometres away, varying the decoration by destination market. The bowl with the date 826 is not a jewel: it is, simply, a product with a serial number. And it is precisely this ordinariness that makes it extraordinary — because it tells the life of everyone, not only of kings.

The treasures

The Tang gold and the birth of blue-and-white

Alongside the mass of bowls, the cargo hid exceptional pieces. The most famous is an octagonal gold cup, the largest of the Tang era ever found: on its sides are engraved musicians and dancers with clearly foreign features, witnesses to how cosmopolitan the Chinese capital was at the time, a metropolis full of faces come from the west.

But the discovery richest in future is more modest: some of the oldest known 'blue-and-white' dishes. That decoration we now associate with Chinese porcelain par excellence was in fact born of an encounter: the white is Chinese ceramic, but the blue is cobalt imported from the Middle East. The most "Chinese" porcelain in the world is, at root, a mixed product — and Belitung preserves one of its earliest proofs.

The open question

Saved, or plundered?

On this wreck weighs a controversy it is right to tell in full. The excavation was not conducted by archaeologists, but by a German commercial company, Tilman Walterfang's Seabed Explorations, with a regular Indonesian permit — because Indonesia is not party to the UNESCO convention that bans the commercial exploitation of submerged sites. The entire cargo was then sold to Singapore for 32 million dollars. In 2011 a major exhibition planned at the Smithsonian was postponed over protests from archaeologists.

An archaeological site is excavated only once. Doing it to sell, and not to know, erases forever information that no catalogue will give back. THE ACADEMIC ARCHAEOLOGY CRITIQUE OF COMMERCIAL SALVAGE

The defence, however, is not trivial. The site was a few metres deep, exposed to looters; the cargo was kept together instead of being dispersed on the market, the ship's structure was documented, and the finds were conserved, studied and published. An imperfect compromise between salvage and science — which this card records without absolving or condemning, because loss and salvage, here, live side by side.

The ship

The seasonal wind

The Belitung dhow was a medium-tonnage cargo ship — a keel of about fifteen metres — with the classic triangular lateen sail that let it sail close to the wind. Not a warship nor a luxury vessel: a working tool, built for one thing, to ride the monsoons between two worlds, and able to do so with a hull held together by cord.

Real finds and images

The collection — the Changsha bowls, the gold cup, the blue-and-white dishes — is held in Singapore and has been exhibited and published; documentation and images are held by museums and cultural institutions.

A note on method

What this page holds together

Two things usually kept apart. The wonder: a wreck that proves, with sixty thousand objects, that the world was interconnected twelve hundred years ago, and that the most "Chinese" porcelain is born of a Middle Eastern pigment. And the unease: we know that treasure because it was recovered commercially and sold, not excavated by science. The two do not cancel each other out, and the page hides neither.

The dates are anchored to an inscription and a laboratory result, not to a legend. And the object that moves us most is not the gold cup, but an ordinary bowl with a date on it: the involuntary receipt of a shipwreck, and the signature of an age we thought we knew only from books.
